## Context

Luntriqa builds tailored technology for insurers and brokers across Latin America. One of its products is the platform where its corporate clients report, review and settle claims.

Different profiles work there, such as analysts, supervisors and the administrators of each corporation, and each one needs the same information at a different level of detail.

## Challenge

A claim goes through several states before it closes, and the information needed to see how operations were going was spread across lists and reports.

The challenge was to answer the everyday questions in a single view: how many claims there are, how long they take to close, whether the SLA promised to the client is being met, and how much money is still waiting to be paid out.

## Approach

I started from those four questions and turned them into the dashboard's first row: total claims, average time from opening to close, SLA compliance against its target, and the amount pending.

Everything else depends on two filters kept at the top, claim type and period, so any role can narrow the view without leaving the screen.

- One dashboard that adapts to the role, instead of a separate screen for each profile.
- SLA compliance is shown next to its target and the gap still to close, not as a lone percentage.
- The side navigation is cut down to the essentials: dashboard, users and corporations.

## Solution

The analysis is organised in tabs (states, subcategories and trends), so the main view never gets crowded.

In the states tab, a donut chart sums up how claims are distributed and, next to it, the breakdown by state shows each count and its share of the total.

Below, a plain-language line reads the numbers, for example: "More than half of the claims are still pending review". That way, whoever opens the dashboard doesn't have to work out the conclusion alone.

## Learnings

In an operational dashboard, the most valuable thing is not showing more data but putting in order the questions people already ask.

Writing the conclusion next to the chart turned out to matter as much as the chart itself, especially for the profiles who don't live inside the numbers.
